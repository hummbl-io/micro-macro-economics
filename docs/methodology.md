# Methodology

The framework has 6 steps. Each step has a question it must answer. If you
can't answer the question, you don't have an analysis yet — you have a
research agenda.

## Step 1: Define the subject

**Question**: What exactly are you analyzing?

State the subject in one sentence. Then state the **unit of analysis** —
is this a product, a market, an industry, a policy, a technology, a
resource? The unit determines what "micro" and "macro" mean.

Example: "Coffee" is too vague. "The global commodity market for arabica
and robusta coffee" is a subject with a clear unit (a commodity market).

## Step 2: Map the actors (micro)

**Question**: Who are the decision-makers, and what are they optimizing for?

Identify:
- **Producers** — who makes/sells the thing? What are their cost structures?
  What constraints do they face (capacity, capital, regulation)?
- **Consumers** — who buys it? What are they willing to pay? What substitutes
  exist? Is demand elastic or inelastic?
- **Intermediaries** — brokers, platforms, retailers, speculators. What
  friction do they add or remove?
- **Complementors** — who provides adjacent goods/services that affect demand?

For each actor, state their **objective function** in one sentence: "X
maximizes ___ subject to ___."

## Step 3: Map the system (macro)

**Question**: What aggregate forces shape the environment these actors operate in?

Identify:
- **Supply-side aggregates** — total production capacity, input costs, technology
  trends, resource constraints
- **Demand-side aggregates** — total market size, demographic shifts, income
  effects, substitution trends
- **Institutional forces** — regulation, trade policy, taxation, subsidies,
  legal frameworks
- **Monetary/fiscal** — interest rates, exchange rates, government spending
  that affects the subject
- **International** — trade flows, comparative advantage, geopolitical risk

## Step 4: Identify the price mechanism

**Question**: How does price form, and what does it signal?

This is the bridge between micro and macro. Price is where individual
decisions meet aggregate conditions.

- Is the price set by a market (auction, bilateral, posted)?
- Is it regulated, subsidized, or distorted?
- What information does the price carry? What information does it hide?
- Who captures the margin between what consumers pay and what producers receive?

## Step 5: Find the feedback loops

**Question**: How do micro decisions aggregate into macro outcomes, and how do
macro conditions reshape micro incentives?

This is the most important step. Look for:
- **Composition effects** — individual rational decisions that produce
  collectively irrational outcomes (e.g., commodity booms and busts)
- **Policy leakage** — macro interventions that distort micro incentives
  (e.g., price floors creating surpluses)
- **Network effects** — where the value to one actor depends on how many
  others participate
- **Time lags** — micro responses to macro signals that arrive too late,
  producing overshoot and correction cycles

## Step 6: Identify the leverage point

**Question**: If you wanted to change the outcome, where would you intervene?

Map each candidate intervention to its level:
- **Micro interventions** — change actor incentives, reduce information
  asymmetry, alter market structure
- **Macro interventions** — change regulation, trade policy, monetary
  conditions, institutional frameworks
- **Cross-level interventions** — the rare cases where a micro change
  produces macro effects (e.g., a technology that collapses transaction
  costs) or a macro change unlocks micro behavior (e.g., deregulation
  that enables new market entrants)

State the leverage point as: "The highest-leverage intervention is ___
because ___."

## What this framework is NOT

- It is not a forecasting model. It doesn't predict prices or quantities.
- It is not a normative framework. It doesn't tell you what "should" happen.
- It is not complete. It is a checklist that forces you to look at both
  levels before drawing conclusions.
- It is not a substitute for domain expertise. The framework structures
  your thinking; it doesn't fill it in.
