# Coffee: Micro vs Macro Economic Analysis

## 1. Subject

The global commodity market for arabica and robusta coffee — the second
most-traded commodity in the world by value after crude oil. ~125 million
people depend on coffee for their livelihoods, mostly in the developing
world. The market is a textbook case of micro-macro interaction: individual
farming decisions aggregate into global supply cycles, while macro forces
(climate, trade policy, exchange rates) determine whether those farmers
can survive.

## 2. Actors (Micro)

### Producers
~25 million coffee farmers, predominantly smallholders with 1-5 hectare
plots. The top producers are Brazil, Vietnam, Colombia, Indonesia, Ethiopia
[1]. Cost structure is labor-dominant — coffee is hand-picked in most
regions because cherries ripen unevenly, preventing mechanization except
on large Brazilian plantations. Marginal cost is low (the trees produce
annually once mature) but opportunity cost is high — farmers could grow
other crops or migrate.

Objective: "Producers maximize household income subject to land
constraints, weather risk, and volatile world prices."

### Consumers
~1 billion daily coffee drinkers globally. Demand is highly inelastic in
the short run (caffeine dependence, habit) but elastic over years (substitution
to tea, energy drinks, quitting). Consumers in importing countries pay
$3-5 per cup at retail, while producers receive $0.01-0.03 per cup's worth
of green beans [2].

Objective: "Consumers maximize utility (caffeine + taste + ritual) subject
to budget constraints."

### Intermediaries
The value chain has 5-7 layers: farmers → local buyers → processors/mills →
exporters → importers → roasters → retailers. Each layer captures margin.
The roaster-retailer segment captures 60-80% of the final retail price [3].
Speculators and hedge funds trade coffee futures on ICE (Intercontinental
Exchange), adding price volatility unrelated to physical supply/demand.

### Complementors
Coffee machines, café culture, office kitchens, milk/sugar. The rise of
espresso-based drinks (lattes, cappuccinos) created a complement-driven
demand boost — consumers invested in machines and developed taste for
higher-quality preparations, increasing willingness to pay.

## 3. System (Macro)

### Supply-side aggregates
Global production ~170 million 60-kg bags/year [4]. Supply is concentrated
geographically — Brazil alone produces ~40% of arabica. This creates
systemic risk: a single country's weather event can swing global supply
by 10-15%. Coffee trees take 3-5 years to reach productive maturity,
creating long supply response lags.

### Demand-side aggregates
Global demand grows ~2% annually, driven by emerging markets (China, India)
transitioning from tea to coffee [5]. Developed markets are saturated but
trading up (premiumization — specialty coffee, single-origin). Population
growth in consuming countries is the primary demand driver.

### Institutional forces
The International Coffee Agreement (collapsed 1989) previously managed
supply via export quotas. Since its collapse, the market is largely
unmanaged — no OPEC equivalent exists. Fair Trade and certification
schemes (Rainforest Alliance, Organic) provide price floors for
participating farmers but cover <10% of global production [6]. EU
deforestation regulation (EUDR, effective 2025) requires traceability
of coffee to plot of origin — a major compliance burden for smallholders.

### Monetary/fiscal
Coffee is priced in USD on global exchanges. Producer countries with
weak currencies (Ethiopia, Uganda) see exchange rate effects amplify or
dampen local-currency income. When the USD strengthens, producer revenue
in local terms can rise even if world prices are flat.

### International
Coffee exemplifies the "commodity trap" — developing countries export
raw materials at low margins while developed countries capture value in
processing, branding, and retail. Trade terms favor importers: tariffs
on green beans are typically zero in developed markets, while roasted
coffee faces higher tariffs — incentivizing raw export over domestic
value addition [7].

## 4. Price Mechanism

Price is set on the ICE futures exchange (arabica) and London ICE
(robusta). Futures prices are the benchmark for physical trade —
farmers receive the "C price" minus a differential based on origin
quality and logistics.

The price carries information about expected future supply (weather
forecasts, crop estimates) and demand (consumption trends). But it
hides: (a) the cost of production for marginal farmers, (b) the
environmental externalities of monoculture coffee, (c) the labor
conditions at origin.

Margin distribution: a $4 latte gives the farmer ~$0.02-0.04. The
roaster gets ~$0.50, the retailer ~$2.50, milk/sugar ~$0.30, the
remainder is overhead and tax [8]. This margin structure is the
central political fact of the coffee economy.

## 5. Feedback Loops

### The price boom-bust cycle (composition effect)
When coffee prices rise, existing farmers expand and new farmers plant.
But trees take 3-5 years to produce — by the time new supply arrives,
prices have often fallen. The resulting oversupply crashes prices,
farmers abandon coffee or switch crops, supply falls, prices rise again.
This cobweb cycle has operated for over a century and is the defining
dynamic of the coffee market. Individual rational decisions (plant when
prices are high) produce collectively irrational outcomes (chronic
oversupply and price collapse).

### Climate feedback (macro → micro → macro)
Rising temperatures and changing rainfall patterns are reducing suitable
coffee-growing land, particularly for arabica (which requires narrow
temperature bands). This macro force pushes micro decisions — farmers
abandon coffee or migrate to higher altitudes, clearing forest. The
deforestation accelerates climate change, further reducing suitable
land. The EUDR's traceability requirement attempts to break this loop
but imposes compliance costs on the poorest actors.

### Premiumization (micro → macro)
Consumer preference for specialty coffee (single-origin, fair-trade,
light-roast) has created a parallel market where prices are 3-10x the
C price. This micro trend is aggregating into a macro bifurcation:
the commodity market (shrinking, low-margin) vs. the specialty market
(growing, high-margin). The two markets have different price mechanisms,
different actors, and increasingly different geographies.

## 6. Leverage Point

**Dominant level**: interaction — but the macro side is where leverage exists.

**Leverage point**: "The highest-leverage intervention is macro — climate
adaptation funding and trade policy reform — because the micro producer
decisions (what to plant, whether to stay) are downstream of macro forces
(climate suitability, world price levels, trade terms) that individual
farmers cannot influence."

**Why not micro**: Interventions at the micro level (certification, direct
trade, cooperative formation) help individual farmers but cannot shift the
boom-bust cycle, the climate trajectory, or the trade terms that determine
system-wide outcomes. A farmer who gets Fair Trade certification earns
more but is still subject to the C price, the exchange rate, and the
weather.

**Why not pure macro**: Pure macro interventions (supply management via
quotas, price floors) have been tried and failed (the ICA collapse). The
lesson is that macro interventions must account for micro incentives —
farmers will cheat on quotas, overplant under price floors, and adapt
to regulations in ways policymakers don't expect.

**The real leverage**: Cross-level interventions that change the
information and incentive structure at both levels simultaneously —
traceability systems that let consumers pay for verified sustainability,
climate adaptation programs that make coffee viable at current latitudes,
and trade policy that allows producer countries to capture more value
through domestic processing.
