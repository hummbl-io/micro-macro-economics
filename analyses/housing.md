# Housing: Micro vs Macro Economic Analysis

## 1. Subject

The US housing market — the production, financing, and distribution of
residential housing. The largest asset class in the American economy:
~$45T in housing stock, with ~$15T in mortgage debt against it [1].
Housing is simultaneously a consumption good (shelter), an investment
asset (equity accumulation), and a financial instrument (mortgage-backed
securities).

Housing is the canonical interaction-dominant market: individual
household decisions about where to live interact with local zoning,
national interest rates, and global capital flows in ways that produce
the affordability crises visible in every major US city.

## 2. Actors (Micro)

### Producers (Homebuilders)
- **Production builders** (D.R. Horton, Lennar, Pulte): build at scale,
  ~40% of new single-family homes [2]. Cost structure: land (20-25%),
  materials (30-35%), labor (25-30%), overhead/financing (15-20%).
- **Custom/small builders**: build one-off homes, ~30% of new construction.
- **Multi-family developers**: build apartments and condos, financed
  differently (commercial loans, tax credits).

Production is location-fixed — you can't manufacture a house in a cheap
location and ship it to an expensive one. This makes housing economics
fundamentally local, even when demand is national.

Objective: "Builders maximize profit per unit subject to land
availability, labor costs, material costs, and local regulation."

### Consumers (Households)
- **Buyers**: purchase homes with mortgages. The largest financial
  decision most households will make. Demand is driven by household
  formation, income, and credit availability.
- **Renters**: rent from landlords or institutional owners. Renting is
  not a substitute for buying — it's a different market with different
  economics, though the two interact.

Demand is highly interest-rate sensitive: a 1% increase in mortgage
rates reduces purchasing power by ~10%. The 2022-2023 rate hike cycle
(3% → 7% mortgages) reduced home sales by 35% [3].

Objective: "Households maximize housing utility (location + size +
quality) subject to income, credit access, and monthly payment
affordability."

### Intermediaries
- **Real estate agents**: 5-6% commission on sales (under pressure from
  recent lawsuits and NAR settlement). ~1.5M licensed agents in the US [4].
- **Mortgage lenders**: banks, credit unions, non-bank lenders (Rocket,
  United Wholesale). Originate loans, then sell to Fannie/Freddie.
- **iBuyers** (Offerpad, Opennation): algorithmic home buying. Small
  market share, failed to scale.
- **Institutional landlords** (Invitation Homes, American Homes 4 Rent):
  own ~3% of single-family rentals. Controversial but small share.

### Complementors
- **Mortgage finance**: Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA, VA — government-
  sponsored enterprises that buy mortgages from lenders, providing
  liquidity. Without them, the 30-year fixed mortgage wouldn't exist
  at scale.
- **Construction labor**: ~30% of builder cost. Labor shortages in
  construction are structural — fewer young workers entering the trades.
- **Materials**: lumber, steel, copper. Subject to commodity cycles and
  tariff effects.

## 3. System (Macro)

### Supply-side aggregates
- US housing stock: ~145M units, ~130M occupied [1]. New construction:
  ~1.5M units/year (2024), still below the ~2M needed to meet demand [5].
- The US has a structural housing deficit of 3-7 million units, accumulated
  over 15+ years of underbuilding following the 2008 crash.
- Construction productivity has barely improved in 50 years — housing is
  one of the few industries where productivity gains have been negligible.
  Modular and prefab construction promise improvement but have failed to
  capture significant market share.

### Demand-side aggregates
- Millennial household formation: the largest generation is now in prime
  homebuying age (30-42), creating structural demand pressure.
- Immigration: population growth from immigration adds ~1M people/year,
  all needing housing.
- Second homes / investment purchases: ~8% of home purchases are
  non-primary [4]. This adds demand but is cyclical.
- Aging in place: seniors are not downsizing at the rate previous
  generations did, reducing supply turnover.

### Institutional forces
- **Zoning**: the most important institutional force. Single-family zoning
  covers ~75% of residential land in major US cities [6]. This makes it
  illegal to build denser housing (duplexes, triplexes, apartments) in
  most of the country. Zoning is local — city councils and zoning boards
  make decisions that shape national housing supply.
- **Building codes**: safety standards that add 20-30% to construction
  costs. Necessary but cumulative — each code update adds cost without
  removing old requirements.
- **Environmental review** (CEQA in California, NEPA nationally): can
  delay projects by 3-10 years. Used as a weapon by opponents of new
  housing ("NIMBY weaponization").
- **Rent control**: in cities like NYC, SF, LA. Reduces rent for
  current tenants but discourages new construction and reduces
  mobility.

### Monetary/fiscal
- **Interest rates**: the single most powerful macro lever. The Federal
  Reserve sets the short-term rate; mortgage rates follow the 10-year
  Treasury yield. The 2020-2021 zero-rate policy created a housing boom;
  the 2022-2023 hikes created a freeze.
- **Fannie/Freddie**: guarantee ~70% of US mortgages. The government's
  implicit backing of the 30-year fixed mortgage is the largest housing
  subsidy in the world — it doesn't appear in the budget but it shapes
  the entire market.
- **Mortgage interest deduction**: costs the federal government ~$25B/year
  in foregone revenue. Benefits primarily upper-income homeowners in
  high-tax states. A subsidy for buying vs. renting.

### International
- Foreign investment in US real estate: ~$50B/year (2024), concentrated
  in gateway cities (NYC, SF, LA, Miami). Adds demand pressure but is
  a small fraction of the total market.
- Global capital flows: when interest rates are low globally, capital
  flows into real estate. The 2010s saw massive international capital
  inflows into US housing, pushing prices up in major cities.

## 4. Price Mechanism

Housing prices form through local market clearing — there is no national
housing price, only local prices that aggregate to national indices
(Case-Shiller, FHFA).

- **Prices are sticky downward**: homeowners refuse to sell below their
  purchase price, creating price floors. In downturns, transaction
  volume falls before price does.
- **Prices are leveraged**: most homes are bought with 80%+ debt. Price
  changes amplify into equity changes — a 10% price drop can wipe out
  100% of a 10%-down buyer's equity.
- **Prices are local**: a house in San Francisco and a house in Detroit
  are different products in different markets, even though they're both
  "houses." National price indices hide enormous local variation.

The price carries information about local supply/demand, school quality,
crime, commute times, and neighborhood amenities. But it hides: (a) the
regulatory constraint on supply (prices are high because building is
illegal, not because land is scarce), (b) the financial leverage
underlying the price (a price supported by 3% mortgages is fragile), (c)
the racial and class dimensions of who can access the market.

**Margin distribution**: in a typical home sale, the seller captures the
appreciation (tax-free up to $500K for married couples). The agent takes
5-6%, the lender takes origination fees + interest over 30 years, and
the government takes property taxes annually. The largest "margin" in
housing goes to the landowner — appreciation of land value is unearned
increment (Henry George's argument, still relevant).

## 5. Feedback Loops

### The supply-demand mismatch (composition effect)
Households rationally want to live in high-opportunity cities (jobs,
schools, amenities). But existing homeowners in those cities rationally
oppose new construction (protects their property values, neighborhood
character). Individual rational decisions (NIMBYism) produce collectively
irrational outcomes (housing shortages, homelessness, outmigration).
This is the defining feedback loop of US housing economics — the
fallacy of composition in its purest form.

### The rate-price lock-in effect (macro → micro → macro)
When interest rates rise, existing homeowners with 3% mortgages won't
sell (they'd have to buy at 7%). This reduces supply, which keeps prices
high even as demand falls. The macro rate hike was supposed to lower
housing prices, but the lock-in effect prevented the supply response.
Prices stayed flat; transaction volume collapsed. The macro intervention
(monitoring rates) produced an unexpected micro behavior (holding not
selling) that blocked the intended macro outcome (price correction).

### The wealth effect (micro → macro)
When home prices rise, homeowners feel wealthier and spend more
(consumer spending drives 70% of GDP). When prices fall, the reverse.
The micro asset (a house) becomes a macro economic driver through the
wealth effect. This is why the Fed monitors housing prices — they're
not just a market, they're a macroeconomic transmission mechanism.

### The segregation loop (macro → micro → macro)
Historical policies (redlining, single-family zoning, highway
construction) created racial and economic segregation. Segregation
produces unequal school funding (funded by local property taxes).
Unequal schools produce unequal outcomes. Unequal outcomes reinforce
segregation (families move to "good school districts"). The macro
legacy of policy shapes micro location decisions that reproduce the
macro pattern. Breaking this loop requires cross-level intervention
(school funding reform + zoning reform + transit investment).

### The construction cycle (micro → macro → micro)
Builders build when prices are high. But construction takes 12-24
months. By the time new homes arrive, the cycle may have turned. The
2021 housing boom triggered a construction surge; by 2023, those homes
hit a market where rates had doubled and demand had fallen. The cobweb
cycle — same as coffee and semiconductors, but with longer lag times
and larger capital commitments.

## 6. Leverage Point

**Dominant level**: interaction — but local regulation is where leverage
exists.

**Leverage point**: "The highest-leverage intervention is zoning and
land-use reform at the local level because it sits at the seam between
micro household location decisions and macro national housing supply.
Legalizing denser housing (duplexes, triplexes, apartments in
single-family zones) is the single intervention that can address the
structural housing deficit."

**Why not micro**: Individual household decisions (buy vs. rent, where
to live) are rational given the constraints. Telling households to move
to cheaper cities doesn't work — the jobs, schools, and amenities are
in the expensive cities. The micro decision is downstream of the macro
constraint.

**Why not pure macro**: Macro interventions (interest rates, mortgage
subsidies) affect demand but not supply. Lowering interest rates makes
housing more affordable temporarily but drives prices up because supply
can't respond. The fundamental problem is that building housing is
illegal in the places where people want to live. Monetary policy can't
fix a zoning problem.

**The real leverage**: Cross-level interventions:
- **Zoning reform** (local → national): cities that legalize denser
  housing (Minneapolis eliminated single-family zoning in 2019;
  California passed SB 9 allowing lot splits) show that local
  regulatory change can unlock supply. The macro effect (more housing
  nationally) comes from thousands of local micro decisions.
- **Transit-oriented development**: building housing near transit
  reduces both the housing crisis and the climate crisis. A cross-
  level intervention that addresses land use, transportation, and
  emissions simultaneously.
- **School funding reform**: decoupling school quality from local
  property taxes breaks the segregation loop. A macro institutional
  change that reshapes micro location decisions.

Housing is the market where the level-mismatch is most visible and most
consequential. The macro narrative ("housing crisis") is produced by
micro decisions (NIMBY opposition at zoning board meetings). The
leverage is at the seam — local regulation — because that's where
individual preferences meet collective outcomes.
